Per capita expenditure ranges from ₹492 in Uttar Pradesh to ₹6,097 in Arunachal Pradesh, a 12-fold gap. The brightest states are mostly small hilly and north-eastern states, while the large plains states of the north and east sit at the dark end; the median is ₹1,304.

Half the states spend less than ₹1,304 per capita. All of the 8 highest spenders have fewer than 15 million people, and 5 of those 8 are in the Northeast, where small populations and hard terrain raise the cost of reaching each person.

Uttar Pradesh has the 2nd-largest health budget in total but ranks 29th per capita, while Sikkim jumps from 29th to 3rd. Totals mostly measure state size, which is why every spending comparison is reported in per capita terms.

The five biggest budgets (Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu, Rajasthan) make up 38% of all state health spending but serve 46% of the population. The boxes for the Northeastern states are small but bright: together this region spends ₹1,759 per capita, against ₹988 for all 29 states combined.

17 of 29 states spend under ₹1,500 per capita, while a long tail of 4 states spends over ₹4,000. Since the mean (₹1,940) is pulled well above the median (₹1,304), rank-based correlations (Spearman) and log scales are used.

In 2019-20, 22 of 29 states spent less on health than they budgeted, by a median of 10%. Arunachal Pradesh spent only 71% of its Budget Estimate, while West Bengal spent 121%. Budgets therefore overstate the actual expenditure on healthcare in several states.

Across the 10 health outcomes, the highest-spending third of states averaged 70/100, compared to 31/100 for the lowest-spending third. This difference is backed by a strong positive correlation between spending and health outcomes (Spearman's ρ = 0.78, p < 0.001, n = 29).

Across the 9 care indicators, the highest-spending third of states averaged 50/100, compared to 54/100 for the lowest-spending third. There is no significant correlation between spending and care indicators (Spearman's ρ = -0.00, p = 1.00, n = 29).

Care and outcomes are only weakly related across states (ρ = +0.14, p = 0.46, n = 29). 7 states get better-than-median outcomes with less-than-median care, with 6 of them being in the Northeast, which suggests that other factors extending beyond the health system, such as diet, sanitation or women's schooling, also shape outcomes.

Rural areas perform worse than urban ones on infant mortality in 28 of 29 states, with a median gap of 6.8 deaths per 1,000 live births. The exception is Arunachal Pradesh. Increased spending does not clearly narrow the rural-urban gap (ρ = +0.28, p = 0.15, n = 29). The extra money is not obviously reaching mothers and children in rural areas first.

Infant mortality ranges from 4.4 deaths per 1,000 live births in Kerala to 50.4 in Uttar Pradesh, over 11 times higher. Stunting varies from 22.3% (Sikkim) to 46.5% (Meghalaya). Anaemia, on the other hand, is prevalent everywhere: in half of all states at least 66% of young children and 46% of pregnant women are anaemic.

12 of 29 states record fewer girls than the natural range allows, lowest in Goa (838 per 1,000 boys), a pattern consistent with sex-selective abortion (foeticide). This shortfall is not directly explained by health spending (ρ = +0.27, p = 0.16, n = 29) or women's schooling (ρ = -0.24, p = 0.22, n = 29). Some of the best-educated states, such as Goa and Himachal Pradesh, have the lowest ratios, which points to a preference for the male child and access to sex-determination technology. Survey estimates for small states are imprecise.

All 10 health outcomes are clearly better in states that spend more, with the strongest link for women with low BMI. But only 1 of the 9 care indicators rises with spending (adequate diet 6–23 m). One result moves the opposite way: families pay more out of their own pocket for a delivery in higher-spending states. A dot whose bar crosses the middle line has no clear link either way.

Mothers and children are far healthier in the quarter of states that spend the most than in the quarter that spends the least: on mothers' health they score 72 out of 100, against 23. But they do not receive more care: the big spenders score lower on care in pregnancy and birth (43 vs 56) and about the same on care for young children (52 vs 51).

Kerala does 33 points better than its spending of ₹1,910 per capita would predict, the best value for money of any state, while Gujarat does 20 points worse than expected on ₹973. 13 of 29 states beat their prediction. How well money is used matters as well as how much is spent.

In the left panel, the states climb with spending: higher-spending states have clearly healthier mothers and children (ρ = +0.77). In the right panel, however, they do not: how much antenatal care, facility births and vaccination families receive is unrelated to spending (ρ = -0.08). The reason is partly the six small north-eastern states, which spend a lot but have little formal care. Without them, care does rise with spending (ρ = +0.44).

Higher-spending states also tend to do better on women's schooling (ρ = +0.45), which could be the real reason their mothers and children are healthier. However, when states with similar women's schooling are compared, spending still matters: the link falls only from +0.77 to +0.72 (p < 0.001), keeping about 93% of its strength.

States that spend more may also have advantages such as better education or cleaner water, which could partly explain their better health outcomes. To examine this, 5 potential influencing factors were tested, one at a time. The relationship remains statistically significant in all checks. The correlation (ρ) between spending and health outcomes is +0.77 before adjustment and ranges from +0.52 to +0.80 after accounting for these factors. The weakest association occurs when women's literacy is taken into account.

The main result is not driven by a few unusual states or by the pandemic: higher spending goes with healthier mothers and children in all three groups (ρ = +0.77 for all states, +0.68 without the north-east and +0.76 for the 18 states surveyed before COVID). Care, on the other hand, behaves differently: once the north-east is left out, higher spending does go with more care (ρ = -0.08 → +0.44), likely because those states spend a lot but have low use of hospitals and clinics.

One might expect families to pay less where the state spends more, but the opposite happens (ρ = +0.70, p < 0.001): a delivery in a public facility costs families ₹1,619 in Madhya Pradesh but ₹14,518 in Manipur. This still holds without the north-eastern states (ρ = +0.58). A possible reason is that care costs more in remote and hilly states, although this claim cannot be backed up by the available data.

How the analysis was done

Data sources

Health expenditure data for 2019-20 were obtained from the RBI's State Finances: A Study of Budgets. Health outcomes and healthcare utilisation indicators were sourced from NFHS-5 (2019-21), covering 29 states. NFHS figures are reported separately for Total, Urban and Rural areas; most of the analysis uses the Total figures.

Data preparation

18 empty columns were removed, the two-row header was consolidated, and 83 values containing Indian-style comma separators were converted to numeric format. No duplicate rows were identified. Potential outliers were examined using five methods: IQR, modified z-score, Grubbs' test, DBSCAN and LOF. Flagged values were retained as plausible official observations. The 72 missing values were imputed using correlation-guided KNN, which estimates each gap from the most similar states on closely related indicators. Mean and median imputation were not used, as they would pull every gap towards a typical value and erase the differences between states.

Per capita expenditure

Total state health expenditure was divided by the corresponding population to calculate per capita spending. This enables comparisons across states of different sizes. A logarithmic scale was used to account for the skewed distribution of spending.

Composite indices

Indicators were normalised to a 0-100 scale, with higher scores representing more favourable outcomes. Indicators where lower values are preferable were reverse-scaled. The Outcome Index averages 7 indicators covering mortality, nutrition and anaemia. The Care Index averages 6 indicators covering maternal care and child healthcare utilisation.

Statistical analysis

Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was used to assess associations between spending and health indicators. Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05. Partial Spearman correlations examined potential confounding factors individually. Robustness checks excluded the six north-eastern states and restricted the analysis to pre-pandemic NFHS-5 Phase 1 states.

Limitations

Correlation does not imply causation

The analysis covers 29 states using cross-sectional data. The observed associations do not establish causality. Factors such as income, education, governance and infrastructure may influence both spending and health outcomes.

Health expenditure coverage is limited

The expenditure measure covers revenue spending under Medical and Public Health. It excludes capital investment, central and private health expenditure, and may omit relevant spending recorded under other budget heads, such as Family Welfare.

Survey timing differs across states

NFHS-5 was conducted in two phases, with Phase 2 extending into the COVID-19 pandemic. All 29 states were retained for national coverage. Results for the 18 Phase 1 states remain similar (ρ = +0.76, compared with +0.77 for the full sample). Expenditure data refer to 2019-20.

Maternal mortality is not directly measured

NFHS-5 does not provide direct estimates of maternal mortality. Available estimates from the Sample Registration System do not offer comparable coverage across all states. Maternal health is therefore assessed indirectly through indicators such as anaemia and nutrition.

Imputed values introduce uncertainty

A total of 72 missing values were imputed using KNN, including 52 in Urban data. Imputed values are estimates, not direct observations. Only 6 missing values occurred in the Total data used in most of the analysis.

State-level estimates vary in precision

Estimates for smaller states, particularly Urban indicators, may be less precise because of smaller survey samples. The six north-eastern states also show distinctive spending and outcome patterns. They were retained in the main analysis and examined separately in robustness checks.

Sex ratio at birth is imprecise for small states

NFHS-5 estimates the sex ratio at birth from births in the five years before the survey, so for small states it rests on relatively few births and has a wide margin of error. For Goa, NFHS-5 reports 838 girls per 1,000 boys, while the Civil Registration System, which records almost all births in the state, reports 947 for 2020. Himachal Pradesh shows a similar gap (875 vs 936). State rankings on this indicator should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Geographical coverage is restricted

The analysis covers 29 states, counting Jammu and Kashmir, and excludes the other Union Territories, such as Delhi and Ladakh. Findings should therefore be interpreted as state-level comparisons, not a complete assessment of all Indian regions.
